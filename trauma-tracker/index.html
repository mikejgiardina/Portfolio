01

The problem it is actually solving

A trauma resuscitation generates an enormous amount of documentable fact in a very short window, and every person in the room is busy. The record gets reconstructed afterward, from memory, by someone who was also doing the resuscitation — which is where timestamps drift, quality measures get missed, and charges go unsupported.

the window

Nobody can document during a resus

The team is doing the work. Whatever is written afterward is a reconstruction, and reconstructions lose exactly the thing that matters most here — when something happened relative to everything else.

the consequence

Three separate deliverables, one source

The nursing narrative, the accreditation clocks, and the charge capture all draw on the same events. Reconstructed separately, they disagree, and a disagreement between them is the thing an auditor finds.

the approach

Decide and produce, don't display

The agents do not surface a dashboard for someone to interpret later. They extract, compute, determine and compose as the resuscitation runs, so the deliverable exists at disposition rather than starting then.

the honesty rule

Gaps ride through to the report

Unresolved prompts are not dropped and not guessed. They travel with the record and appear in the output as documented gaps — which is the only version of this that is safe to put in front of a clinician.

02

What the agents do

From a live trauma-bay transcript, each one owns a decision rather than a view.

Extraction, timestamped as spoken

Values and interventions are pulled out as they are said and stamped at that moment — not at the moment someone gets around to charting them. The ordering is the clinical record in a resuscitation.

Scores computed deterministically

Shock Index, RTS and GCS are calculated from the extracted values as the patient changes, using published coding tables. Deterministic, not inferred — a score a model guessed at is not a score anyone can defend.

Charges gated on their own documentation

A charge fires only when the documentation that code requires is actually present. This is the inverse of the usual failure: rather than billing and hoping the record supports it, the record is what permits the charge to exist at all.

Clocks and activation, with the evidence cited

Accreditation clocks run and the trauma-activation determination is made against explicit criteria, and the determination carries the evidence it was made on, so a reviewer can check the reasoning rather than the conclusion.

A narrative composed one sentence per event

The signable nursing narrative is written as the resus runs, a sentence at a time, in step with what actually happened. Not summarised at the end from a pile of fields.

03

What comes out

A multi-page report at disposition, in four parts.

SectionWhat it carries
Nursing narrativeSignable, composed during the resuscitation rather than after it.
Charge & complianceOnly charges whose required documentation is present, with the documentation attached.
Activation determinationThe criteria applied, the evidence cited, and the resulting determination.
Post-case debriefSurfaces only what is genuinely worth passing on, not a transcript dump.

And the gaps. Anything the system asked about and did not get an answer to appears in the report as an unresolved prompt. A documentation tool that quietly fills its own gaps is worse than one that leaves them visible — the same argument the enforcement write-up makes about checks that report success without having run.

04

Where this came from

Built at the Abridge × Anthropic × Lightspeed agentic-AI hackathon on July 18, 2026 (Pacific time), in a single build window. Two repositories are involved and they are not the same thing.

1The submissionpublic · archived · frozen

The hackathon submission is ttprod, archived and frozen exactly as submitted. The event ran under a new-work-only rule with judge-visible commit timestamps, and every commit in that repository falls inside the build window. Before the event I prepared reference data, prompt drafts, synthetic trauma-bay scripts and planning notes. The application itself, the engine, the capture layer, the live board and the report, was built on the day. It is named because a provenance claim you cannot check is not a provenance claim.

2This buildpublic · MIT · same build, merged afterward

One build session produced two working versions of the app. The submission video shows one of them. The other, built in the same session, is the version I demoed to the judge, and it is the one published here; its first commit is from the evening of the event. Four days later, the features that existed only in the other version were merged in, the model-call plumbing was reworked, and a set of fixes and refinements followed. That is the only post-event work on the application itself, and the commit history is public if you want the exact instants.

On the sponsor names. Naming the event is factual attribution and nothing more. Beyond judging it at the event under competition rules, no sponsor has reviewed, validated or endorsed this repository or the product.

How it was built

Same as everything else here: I specified the clinical behavior, directed the agents that implemented it, and own whether the output is right — which for this project means whether a charge fires when it should not, whether a clock starts on the wrong event, and whether a narrative sentence describes what actually happened. I did not type the implementation and do not claim to.

The clinical judgment is the part that is mine and the part that is hard to hire: knowing that a score has to be deterministic to be defensible, that a charge must be gated on its own documentation rather than asserted alongside it, and that an unanswered prompt has to survive into the report instead of being resolved away.
