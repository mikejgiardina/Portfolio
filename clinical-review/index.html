Clinical AI evaluation

Two reviewers agreed. That was the problem.

A review pipeline for clinical education content runs two AI reviewer personas over every article and keeps only what both of them independently flag. The intersection is the quality signal. This is the write-up of what happened when I measured whether that signal was real.

It was not. The two reviewers made the same mistake on the same sentence, and agreement promoted that error into the highest-confidence bucket available.

The numbers come from the review tool's own output on my clinical-education articles, not a benchmark. Section 03 uses a 79-article review set; section 04 covers all 93 articles published at the time.

01

How the review works

What the two reviewers were set up to do.

Two reviewers with different expertise

One AI reviewer is set up as a board-certified emergency physician. The other is set up as a specialist in the article's subject. They run separately and never see each other's notes.

Each reads two documents

The article itself, and a formal companion version of it that carries the reference list. The companion is never published. It is there so a reviewer can check whether a claim in the article is supported by its source.

Every finding quotes the text

Each finding has a severity (blocker, major or minor), a category, and the exact words being flagged. The quote is what lets two separate reviews be compared.

Matching by quoted text, not a third AI

Two findings count as agreeing when they quote substantially the same passage. A third AI model refereeing would be one more opinion that needs checking, and it would likely have shared the blind spot described below.

Method details

Overlap is the highest of three text-similarity scores (sequence ratio, token-set Jaccard and containment), with 0.7 as the cutoff. Containment is there because one reviewer may quote a phrase where the other quotes the whole sentence. The check can be done by hand and gives the same answer every time.

02

When agreement stops being evidence

This finding changed how I read the reviewers' output.

The companion documents include correction notes: passages that quote an older version of a claim, then explain why it was wrong and what replaced it. They read like "the article originally said X … This is incorrect."

Both reviewers read that note, picked up the old, already-corrected wording, and reported it as an error in the current article. That wording is no longer in the article or its sources. It exists only in the record of a fix already made.

what the matching did with it

Both reviewers made the same mistake on the same words, so the quotes matched exactly and the finding was ranked as high-confidence as the tool allows.

Two reviewers agreeing only means something when they can be wrong in different ways. Here the mistake came from a document both of them read, so their agreement made a wrong finding look certain. A third reviewer reading the same document would most likely have agreed too.

So agreement now gets read two ways. On clinical content, two reviewers agreeing is a strong signal. On citation and sourcing claims it is a weak one, because both reviewers are reading the same companion document and can be misled by it the same way.

03

What 227 blockers were actually reporting

A raw count of findings said very little about the content.

227 of the 238 blockers were the same kind: a claim in the article that the companion's references did not support. In 69% of all agreed findings, the reviewer said outright that it had not been given the companion document.

With nothing to check against, citation blockers ran 3.1 times higher. The reviewer was saying "I can't verify this," and the tool was recording it as "this is wrong." Those are two different statements, and the tool had one place to put both.

181 of those findings came down to one fix: at the time, 44 published articles had no companion document in their source material. Working through the findings one by one would have meant weeks of edits to text nobody had shown was wrong.

Where these numbers come from. This section uses a 79-article review set from two runs about ten weeks apart, when 88 articles were published. Combining the two runs was a mistake: the older findings were written against text that had since been rewritten. The rates are quoted for what they show about the tool, not as a current measurement. Section 04 uses a different, current set.

04

The gate certified the wrong thing

The check could not tell a review that ran from a review someone acted on.

The check before publishing blocked an article only when its review was missing. An article with every finding present, unread and unaddressed passed. It confirmed that a review had happened, not that anyone had done anything about it.

Now every finding that can block publishing needs a recorded decision: fixed, rejected, not applicable, or deferred. Nobody has to agree with the reviewer. Someone has to decide, and say why.

Reasons have a minimum

Every decision other than fixed needs a written reason of at least 20 characters, which rules out "n/a" and "wontfix".

Deferrals expire

A deferral needs a review-by date and blocks publishing once that date passes. One group of deferred findings had sat untouched through several rounds of work and turned out to be real defects.

The citation rule came from the numbers

Citation findings block publishing only when the article has a companion document. Dropping citation findings entirely looked like the easy answer and would have been wrong: the worst defect in a recent batch, a figure misstated in a way that made a situation sound more urgent, was filed as a citation finding, on an article that did have a companion.

When reviewers disagree, it blocks

If either reviewer's category would block, the finding blocks. Findings with no category block too. When the two disagree about what kind of problem it is, the tool holds the article.

what tightening it showed

Turning the fix on took the blocked count from 4 articles to 71 of 93. Nothing was exempted to make that look better. Those 71 articles had unaddressed findings the day before too; the stricter check only made them visible.

Working through them took six passes and produced a decision on all 180 blocking findings: 131 not applicable (a missing companion, or an objection that was preference rather than a defect), 42 fixed, and 7 rejected because making the change would have introduced an error.

what was actually in there

16 real clinical defects, found and corrected. The blocked count went back to 4 of 93, and those four are existing holds on wording with nothing clinical in them.

The defects were not stylistic. Several were dosing: a benzodiazepine underdose for status epilepticus, in an article that contradicted another article in the same library that had the right number; a neonatal dextrose dose carried over from the infant range; a pediatric bradycardia instruction that had dropped both of its qualifying conditions; an eclampsia article with no magnesium regimen in it at all. One was a guideline attributed to a trial whose primary endpoints were negative. The point is not that the reviewers were often right. Sixteen defects were still sitting in a system nobody had checked, and they came out only because I measured it.

05

What this shows

None of this shows the review tool is good. Everything above is a way it was wrong, found by measuring it. What it does show: a review tool nobody has checked gives you confidence without information, and the difference does not show until someone checks.

All three findings had the same shape. The tool was measuring something close to what we believed it measured, and each time the gap led to the wrong conclusion.

The clinical judgment is the part that is mine: knowing that "I can't verify this" and "this is wrong" cannot share one answer when a clinician is reading it, that a correction note is not an error, and that a deferral with no date means nobody will look again. I specified how the tool should behave, and AI coding tools built it. I did not write the code and do not claim to.

Clinical review tool · internal tooling for a clinical-education library · no patient data of any kind.
