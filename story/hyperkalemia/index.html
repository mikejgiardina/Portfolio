A Heart at the Threshold

Potassium is climbing, and the ECG will tell you before the lab does. The monitor above is his — watch the waveform bend as the number rises, and snap back when you treat it.

Adapted from the Season 2 scholarly article · Mike Giardina BSN, RN, CEN
Scroll to begin the case▼
00:00 Triage · arrives by wheelchair

She's forty-nine and she apologizes for coming in. Missed her dialysis run on Tuesday — the ride fell through — and now her legs feel like sandbags and her lips won't stop tingling. She's calm. Too calm for what her heart is doing.

You get her onto the monitor before anything else, and the trace stops you: the QRS complexes are ordinary enough, but the T waves are tall — narrow, tented, pulling up to a point like circus tents. Her pulse is 56 and lazy. "I feel fine," she says. "Just tired."

◇ The waveform is the first lab

Those peaked T waves — narrow-based, symmetrical, tented — are the earliest ECG sign of hyperkalemia, and they show up before any blood comes back. In a dialysis patient who missed a session, that trace is a diagnosis you can act on now.

The rule the article is blunt about: a patient with reason to be hyperkalemic (renal failure, weakness, missed dialysis) gets an ECG immediately, and if the T waves are peaked, you treat before the lab confirms it. Don't wait for the number.

00:04 Why the tent?

It's worth knowing what you're looking at, because it tells you where this is heading. Every one of her heart cells is sitting closer to its trigger than it should be.

◇ The narrowing safety margin

A resting cardiac cell sits at about −90 mV, and it fires at roughly −60 mV. That 30 mV gap is the safety margin — the buffer between resting and firing. Potassium sets that resting voltage (Nernst): as serum K⁺ rises, the resting potential drifts up toward threshold, and the buffer shrinks.

Shrink the margin and you get the whole progression in order: peaked T (twitchy repolarization) → flattened P + widened QRS (conduction slows) → sine wave → asystole. The ECG is a voltmeter for how close she is to the edge.

00:09 iSTAT back · K⁺ 7.3

The point-of-care gas prints and there it is: potassium 7.3. On the monitor, the P waves have gone shy and the QRS is starting to smear wider at the edges — the tent is becoming something worse. Heart rate drifting into the low 50s.

The resident reaches for the insulin. You stop them, because the order here is not the order you'd guess.

◇ Wide QRS, K⁺ 7.3, rate in the 50s — what goes in first?
00:12 Calcium · and a lesson in what it does

Ten mL of 10% calcium gluconate, slow IV push over three minutes. You watch the monitor while it goes in, and within a couple of minutes the trace loosens — the QRS pulls back in, the T waves sit down, the P waves come back to visit. The heart looks, on screen, almost reassured.

Now look at the K⁺ tile. Still 7.3.

◇ The most misunderstood drug in the room

Calcium did not lower her potassium by a single point — and it was still exactly the right first move. Calcium raises the firing threshold back away from that drifted-up resting voltage, re-widening the safety margin. It buys the heart 30–60 minutes of protection while the drugs that actually lower K⁺ get to work.

That split — a calmer ECG sitting on top of an unchanged number — is the whole mental model of hyperkalemia. Onset 1–3 minutes; it is the first thing you give for any hyperkalemia with ECG changes. It is a shield, not a cure.

00:22 Now actually move the potassium

Shield up, you go after the number. Two drugs to shove potassium back inside the cells where 98% of it belongs — and one pitfall you say out loud so nobody forgets it.

◇ Shift therapy — temporary, but fast

  • Insulin 10 units IV + D50 25 g — drives K⁺ into cells via the Na-K pump. Onset 10–20 min; drops K⁺ 0.5–1.2 mEq/L. Works even with no kidneys.
  • Albuterol 10–20 mg neb — same pump, different key. Additive with insulin. Onset 20–30 min.

The pitfall, said aloud: insulin without dextrose will lower her potassium and then drop her sugar into the floor. Always give the glucose, and check a fingerstick afterward — insulin outlasts a single amp of D50, so the hypoglycemia can arrive an hour later.

And know what shift buys you: it's a loan, not a payment. The potassium is hidden inside cells, not gone from the body. It will leak back out.

◇ The map is not the territory

ECG changes track K⁺ loosely, not exactly. Some patients sit at 7+ with only tall T waves; others show a wide QRS at 6. Chronicity, calcium, and magnesium all bend the curve. So the ECG tells you how unstable this heart is right now — which is what you actually treat — while the serum number tells you how much potassium you have to remove. You need both.

00:40 Three jobs, not one

By now her monitor looks almost normal and her potassium is trending down — but "trending down" is not "fixed," and the resident asks what's left to do. The answer is easier if you stop treating hyperkalemia as one problem. It's three jobs, and every drug does exactly one of them.

Protect · Shift · Remove — sort the shelf

Tap each therapy. It drops into the one job it actually does.

◇ Protect
Guards the heart. Changes no K⁺.
◇ Shift
Hides K⁺ in cells. Temporary.
◇ Remove
Takes K⁺ out of the body. Definitive.
Only the Remove column ends the emergency. Everything else is buying time until it happens.
00:55 The definitive move

Insulin and albuterol bought the hour. Calcium bought the minutes. But she has no kidneys to pee the potassium out and no bowel trick fast enough to trust — so the only thing that actually ends this is the machine she missed on Tuesday. Nephrology writes for urgent dialysis, and because she's a chronic HD patient the access is already hers — the fistula thrills under your fingers, nothing to place — so they can run her now.

Dialysis pulls the potassium out for real. On the monitor, the trace has forgotten it was ever a tent: ordinary P, tidy QRS, a T wave that sits back down where it belongs. Rate in the 70s. K⁺ 4.8.

◇ Removal is the only cure in ESRD

Hemodialysis is definitive for total-body hyperkalemia — it clears 1–2+ mEq/L and doesn't care whether she makes urine. Calcium protected, insulin and albuterol stalled, but in a dialysis-dependent patient only removal closes the case. Watch for rebound: potassium leaks back out of cells as the shift wears off, so the number can climb again after the run.

01:30 Off to the unit

Ninety minutes ago her heart was a few millivolts from quitting, and she apologized for the inconvenience. The save was never one dramatic drug — it was reading the waveform before the lab, shielding the membrane, stalling with the shift, and removing the load. In that order. Protect, shift, remove.

You watch the T wave sit quietly on the screen. Ordinary. Which, tonight, is the most beautiful shape it could make.

The waveform did the teaching.
