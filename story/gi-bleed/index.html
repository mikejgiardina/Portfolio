Bay 4 Is Bleeding

A gastrointestinal hemorrhage, told in real time — from the doorway smell of melena to the endoscopy suite. The monitor at the top is his. Watch it as you read.

Adapted from the Season 2 scholarly article · Mike Giardina BSN, RN, CEN
Scroll to begin the case▼
00:00 Triage · walk-in

He walks in on his own two feet, which is the first thing that fools everyone. Fifty-eight, pale as the floor, a plastic grocery bag clutched to his chest. He sets it on the triage desk without a word and opens the top so you can see inside: dark, coffee-ground vomit, and streaks of red that are unmistakably not coffee.

"Started this morning," he says. "Thought it'd pass." His skin is cool. His shirt collar is loose on a neck that used to fill it. On his abdomen, when you lift the shirt, a faint web of spider veins and a belly that's rounder than the rest of him.

◇ What the bag is telling you

Hematemesis — vomited blood — plus melena (black, tarry, digested blood) points upstream: an upper GI bleed, proximal to the ligament of Treitz. Upper sources account for roughly 80% of clinically significant GI hemorrhage.

The spider angiomas and distended abdomen are doing quiet diagnostic work. They whisper cirrhosis — and in a cirrhotic who is vomiting blood, you assume esophageal varices until an endoscope proves otherwise. Variceal bleeds are brisk, massive, and carry a 30-day mortality of 15–30%.

00:04 Room 4 · first vitals

Back in the room, the numbers arrive and they don't match the calm man on the stretcher. Heart rate 104. Pressure 104 over 78 — the diastolic is up, the pulse pressure narrowing like a fist closing. He answers your questions, but a beat slow, and twice he asks where his wife is when she's standing right there.

◇ The body is lying to protect him

A "normal-ish" blood pressure early in hemorrhage is a trap. Young, healthy compensation can hold systolic pressure until 30–40% of blood volume is gone — then it falls off a cliff. Read the compensation, not the systolic:

CLASS IHR <100 · BP normal · alert<15%
CLASS IIHR 100–120 · narrowing pulse pressure · anxious15–30%
CLASS IIIHR >120 · systolic drops · confused30–40%
CLASS IVHR >140 · SBP <90 · obtunded>40%

Tachycardia, a narrowed pulse pressure, and new confusion put him in Class II sliding toward III. The slow answers aren't fear — they're the first sign of cerebral hypoperfusion.

00:09 Access & the first fork

You go for access before you go for anything else. Two large-bore IVs — 18 gauge, one in each AC — because the size of the tube decides how fast you can give back what he's losing. Type and cross, CBC, coags, lactate, a full chem. The tech is already wheeling the ultrasound over.

Then the resident says the thing that used to be reflex: "Let's open up fluids wide, two liters." You pause with your hand on the bag.

◇ Your move — what runs through those IVs first?
00:16 He crashes

It happens the way it always happens — between one set of vitals and the next. He turns his head and brings up a full emesis basin of bright red blood, and the monitor screams before you do. HR 131. Systolic 82. His lips go the color of the walls.

"Massive transfusion — go."

The cooler comes up from the blood bank. You hang the first unit of red cells and someone starts the count, because from here it's a ratio, not a guess.

◇ Balanced resuscitation — replace blood with blood

He is now Class IV. Crystalloid alone would dilute what few clotting factors he has left and drop his temperature — feeding the lethal spiral of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy. So you give back what he's losing, in the proportion he's losing it:

1 : 1 : 1
PRBC : FFP : PLATELETS

Permissive hypotension is the counterintuitive heart of it: aim for a systolic of just 80–90 mmHg while he's actively bleeding. Push the pressure higher and you pop the fragile clots forming at the varix. Keep it low enough to perfuse brain and kidney, no higher, until the source is sealed.

00:24 The right drugs, now

While the products run, you build the pharmacology around the working diagnosis. Cirrhotic, likely variceal — so this is not a plain ulcer, and the orders reflect that. You call them back as you draw them up.

◇ Orders that match the source

  • Octreotide — 50 mcg IV bolus, then 50 mcg/hr. Somatostatin analog; drops portal pressure and slows the variceal bleed.
  • Ceftriaxone — 1 g IV. Prophylactic antibiotics in a cirrhotic GI bleed measurably cut infection and mortality.
  • Pantoprazole — 80 mg IV bolus, then 8 mg/hr infusion. If any of this turns out to be ulcer, a gastric pH >6 stabilizes the clot.

Note what's not reflexive here: the PPI is the star for peptic ulcers, but in a variceal bleed it rides shotgun. Octreotide and antibiotics are the mortality movers. Match the drug to the source, not to the habit.

00:31 Putting a number on the danger

The GI fellow is on the phone and asks the question every consultant asks: "How sick is he, really?" You don't hand them a vibe. You hand them a score — the same one the literature uses to decide who can go home and who cannot.

◇ Why score at all

A Glasgow-Blatchford of 0 is the rare green light for discharge and outpatient scope. Our patient — hypotensive, tachycardic, melena, known liver disease — lands high. The score doesn't tell you anything your gut didn't; it tells the next clinician, the bed manager, and the chart, in a language that travels.

00:48 The definitive move

Resuscitation buys minutes; it doesn't fix pipes. The only thing that stops a variceal bleed is getting eyes and a band on the varix. GI is scrubbing. He's held at a systolic in the low 100s now, mentating again, the cell-saver ratio holding his coags together.

You wheel him to the suite yourself — NPO confirmed, a second unit checked and hanging, suction tested, the airway cart at the head of the bed because a stomach full of blood is an aspiration waiting to happen. "Ready when you are," the endoscopist says. The scope goes down. On the screen: a column of dilated veins, one of them weeping. A band snaps closed around it. The weeping stops.

◇ Timing is the therapy

Urgent endoscopy — within the first hour or two — is for the unstable, actively-bleeding patient like this one; it is both the diagnosis and the treatment. Band ligation achieves hemostasis in 80–90% of variceal bleeds. Your job in the doorway to the suite is airway, access, and blood — endoscopy can't work on a patient who's aspirated or arrested on the way.

Now — and only now that the source is controlled — the pressure target comes up. Permissive hypotension ends when the bleeding does.

01:20 Handoff to the ICU

Eighty minutes from the grocery bag to a banded varix. He's going to the unit, not the morgue, and the difference was never one heroic act — it was a dozen ordinary ones done in the right order: two good IVs, blood instead of saline, a pressure held deliberately low, the right three drugs, a number on the danger, and a clean road to the scope.

You watch the monitor settle. HR in the 80s. A real systolic, earned honestly. You clean the room. Somewhere out front, someone else is walking in on their own two feet.

Same medicine. Different doorway.
