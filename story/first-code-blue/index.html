Sink or Swim

Surviving your first code blue — told through the one monitor nobody ever shows you. Your own. The vitals up top are yours; steady them as you read.

The original essay, staged.
Written by Mike Giardina, BSN, RN, CEN
Begin▼
Intro The physiology of the provider

The first time a new nurse hears someone scream "I NEED HELP!" or "GRAB THE CART!", the physiological response is nearly identical to that of the patient they're about to treat. Heart rate spikes or drops to the floor, peripheral vision narrows, fine motor skills deteriorate. Knees weak. Arms heavy. There might even be vomit on the sweater already. This is fight or flight at its core.

It's an instinctual response — a survival mechanism evolved for running from predators, never made for calculating epinephrine intervals or measuring the depth of chest compressions. We spend cumulative months on mannequins that don't bleed, vomit, or have grieving families screaming in the corner. The gap between simulation and reality is where many new graduates falter.

◇ Look up. That's your monitor.

This piece isn't a guide to what to give and when. Plenty of things can teach you the algorithm. What nothing teaches you is the part that decides whether you can run the algorithm: your own nervous system, redlining before you've touched the patient.

The first pulse you need to check is your own — before you walk through the door.

Phase 1 The pre-code pause

The most critical moment of a resuscitation often happens before you touch the patient — the ten seconds it takes to walk from the station to the bay. In that window you perform a self-triage. Arrest with a couple-minute ETA? Time to crack the cart and set up your laboratory. Epi. Amio. Bicarb. Modern alchemy at its finest.

If you enter a chaotic room at a heart rate of 140, you're going to be part of the problem, not the solution.

Take a tactical breath · 4-4-4

Box breathing engages the parasympathetic nervous system and widens your cognitive bandwidth. Follow the circle. Watch your monitor.

Ready
Three rounds. You cannot organize a chaotic room if your own internal state is in chaos.
Phase 2 The pit crew — finding your lane

In high-acuity situations we work like a pit crew. No one on the side of a racetrack asks anyone else what to do — they fall in line and claim the role that suits them. Scan for the code lead. Attending, resident, ICU charge — doesn't matter. There's only one question to ask:

"What do you need?"

You'll default to one of three high-value, low-ambiguity roles. Tap the one calling you.

◇ The hazard of hovering

The most dangerous thing a new nurse does is hover — standing near the bed without a task. It adds noise, blocks the leader's line of sight, and physically obstructs the team. If you don't have a role, find one — or go hold down the rest of the department, because the world doesn't stop when this happens.

Phase 3 The mechanics of efficacy

Effective resuscitation is physics and chemistry, not magic. The one number the AHA hammers is compression fraction — the percentage of the code that hands are actually on the chest. Every time you come off, coronary perfusion pressure falls toward zero, and it takes several compressions to build it back.

◇ Compression fraction — keep the pressure up

Every minute of coronary perfusion is a minute of cerebral blood flow restored. It doesn't matter if you restart everything if the heart and brain aren't recoverable.

HANDS ON

The pearl: do not pause compressions for intubation. Do not pause for access. The only pauses are rhythm checks — under 10 seconds — and defibrillation. The first thing on everyone's mind, in every role: compressions stop only to check for a true pulse.

Closed-loop communication

Auditory exclusion is real. Repeat what you heard, out loud, before a mistake is made. Play it through.

Leader: "Give 1 mg epinephrine IV push."
Med nurse: "1 mg epinephrine IV push."
Med nurse: (administers) "1 mg epinephrine is in."
Scribe: marks the time. Order heard, dose verified, action closed.
Phase 4 The fall

The code doesn't end when the pulse comes back or time of death is called. For a new grad, the minutes after are the ambush. The high you felt from that rush of adrenaline will drop you off a cliff. Crashing is the only option if you didn't already pack a parachute — and plenty of nurses have crashed and not gotten back up.

So do it again. The 4-4-4, this time with your eyes closed, clearing your head. Don't internalize what you saw. That exposure builds over each code until it spills over — and there's a better name for it than trauma dumping.

◇ Debrief — and don't do it alone

A five-minute standing huddle right after, standard practice in any high-performing ER. Not blame, not guilt — systems. What went well: "good role assignment," "high compression fraction," "a recommendation I hadn't thought of." What we improve: "delay getting the IO drill," "too much noise," "too long between compressions."

The "stiff upper lip" culture of the past is what feeds burnout and PTSD. Acknowledging that a code was hard is not weakness; it is humanity — and there's no greater loss than losing yours along with someone else's life.

Phase 5 The aftermath

Sometimes, after all of it, death wins. This isn't a textbook, and the order was never the point — every code is different. But if the work ends in a loss, there has to be more than a debrief. There has to be a moment.

It isn't a question of belief. It's respect. The body in front of you was someone's mother, father, child, friend, or spouse — someone who, a couple of hours ago, might have said "see you later" for the last time.

Remove the equipment you can. Clear the caps out of the sink. Clean the floor, even if it's been there for days. Find a couple of chairs and a box of tissues. Sit them up, wash their face and hands, a fresh gown and fresh linens. Treat their body the way you'd want another nurse to treat someone you love. It gives everyone in the room a turning point — from fighting for a life to honoring a death.

If you ever lose the feeling of being humbled by this, consider it time to reach out for more help than you're already getting.

Carry this what the next one already knows

The next code will be a little easier. You'll fill a different role, with a little more confidence, and you'll teach the one standing where you're standing now. Until then, carry these.

Don't panic; breathe.
Don't assume; communicate.
Don't take flight; stay and fight.
Don't forget to respect the situation, the person, and their loved ones.
Don't disconnect; debrief.
And remember that you trained for this.

This is the pinnacle of your skill and profession.

There's no greater honor than taking everything you know and using it to restore as much life as possible to the person who entrusted it to you. Sink, or swim. You already know how to swim — you just have to remember to breathe.

— Mike Giardina, BSN, RN, CEN
