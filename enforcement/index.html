PROCESS · KEY-MAN RISK

Process design · governance

Turning conventions into checks that outlive the author.

A process document that only works because the person who wrote it keeps re-reading it isn't risk mitigation — it's a journal. A process enforced by a failing check survives that person leaving. So I sorted every discipline this workspace runs on into those two piles on purpose, and built a scoreboard for moving items from one pile to the other. When the scoreboard shrinks, key-man risk shrinks — measurably, not rhetorically.

Two artifacts do the work. A register maps every discipline and classifies it. A ledger tracks the conversion work and verifies that the ones already claimed as enforced actually fire. The register is the map; the ledger is the scoreboard. Keeping them separate is the point — a backlog buried inside a reference document stops being a backlog.

01

The two columns most registers leave out

Plenty of projects keep an index of their process docs. It answers “what governs us, and where does it live.” That's useful and it isn't enough, so the register adds four columns — and two of them are the whole reason it exists.

column

Forged by

The specific incident that produced this rule. Every entry names one. A procedure with no incident behind it is process theater — it reads hollow precisely because nothing forced it into existence, and it's the first thing a successor correctly ignores.

column

Enforcement

Enforced, narrated, or hybrid. This is the load-bearing column: the gap between the enforced pile and the narrated pile is the key-man risk, written down where it can be watched shrinking.

column

Owner on handoff

Who owns this when I'm not the one running it. Today most rows say “founder,” and that's the honest answer — but naming the role that inherits each one makes the handoff a delegation exercise instead of an excavation.

discipline

Point, never copy

The register classifies and links; it never holds a procedure's body. If a procedure's content starts getting pasted in, that's the signal to stop and link instead — a rule with two homes has already begun to drift.

The stranger-trap tier. The register also catalogues rules that live as prose inside configuration and context files, never extracted into a standalone document. Those are the ones a newcomer misses completely, because nobody thinks to go looking for a policy inside a config file. They're indexed here so they're findable — and left where they are, because that's where they're enforced.

02

Three states, one honest caveat

Every discipline gets exactly one value. The definitions are deliberately unflattering, because a generous classification is worse than none.

StateMeansSurvives the author leaving?
ENFORCED A test, audit, hook, or CI gate goes red when the procedure is violated. Yes. This is the real asset — the only category that is.
HYBRID Part is checked; part rides on a human reading the prose. Partly. The narrated portion is the exposure — name it explicitly.
NARRATED Prose. Often correct, often battle-forged, but it holds only while someone reads it. No. Degrades the moment attention lapses.

The caveat printed at the top of the column. These values reflect what the source documents claim is enforced. Confirming that each enforced row has a live check which actually goes red — rather than one that silently passes — is itself the first hardening task. Until a row has been walked, it reads “claimed enforced, verify it bites.” That caveat turned out to be load-bearing; see below.

03

What earns the word “enforced”

All four have to be true. The bar is this high because a check that silently passes is worse than no check at all — it reads as coverage you don't have, and it stops anyone from looking again.

It fails red on a real violation

Proven with a known-bad fixture, not assumed from reading the code. If you can't show it failing, it isn't a check yet — it's an intention with a filename.

It does not pass green on a degraded environment

A cold virtual environment, a missing dependency, an absent file: all must produce an explicit error, never a silent pass. The reference pattern resolves through a chain of fallbacks and, when it runs out, degrades to “no result” rather than to a wrong one.

It runs automatically

A CI gate, a session lifecycle hook, or a pre-publish step — not “remember to run it.” A check that depends on being remembered has the same failure mode as the convention it replaced.

Its failure message points at the fix

A red check that doesn't say what to do hasn't removed the tacit knowledge from the founder's head. It has relocated it into a stack trace, where it's harder to find.

Criterion two came from a real incident: an audit's last-resort fallback fired on a cold environment and manufactured a regression of over a thousand findings that did not exist. Nothing was broken except the ability to tell. Chasing a phantom for an afternoon is a cheap lesson in why degrade-to-nothing beats degrade-to-plausible.

04

Three ledgers

The scoreboard splits three ways, because “not yet enforced,” “claims to be enforced,” and “can never be enforced” need three different kinds of attention — and mixing them is how a backlog becomes a wish list.

AConversion backlognarrated → target check

The work, ordered by leverage. Each row names the concrete check to build, where it will live, what it must assert, and what it costs to keep not having it. Highest-leverage items are the ones with direct external exposure — anything that could put a wrong number in front of a grant reviewer or an investor.

Rows are honest about partiality. Several are marked “backlog (partial)” because only the mechanical portion of the discipline can be checked and a judgment residue will always remain. Pretending otherwise would produce a check that passes while the actual rule is broken.

BVerification queueclaimed-enforced → prove it bites

Every check that says it's enforced, with two separate columns: exists (the file is on disk) and bite-tested (a known-bad input has been shown to make it go red). Until the second column is filled in, the check is provisional no matter how good the code looks.

This queue is why the register has that caveat at the top — and it earned its keep on the very first pass through it, twice over.

CWon't enforcejudgment · stop trying

Disciplines that are irreducibly judgment: how to collaborate with an agent, when a change deserves a decision record, whether a given commit is a release, the order to work through a triage. These stay narrated, and that's correct — their mitigation is onboarding and documentation, not a gate.

Writing them down as permanently unenforceable is the point. It stops effort being spent trying, and it stops the ledger from carrying items that will never close, which is how backlogs quietly lose credibility.

05

What the first pass found

The verification queue was built on a suspicion: that some checks the register listed as enforced weren't firing. The first walk through it confirmed that twice, and both were worse than a missing check — because both looked like passing ones.

casualty one · failed to warn

The scanner that examined nothing

A protective scanner read the path of the edited file from environment variables the runtime no longer provided. The path came back empty, so it exited on its first line — every run, silently. Its warning was also written to a stream the wiring discarded, so even when it did have something to say, nobody heard it. Two independent bugs, stacked, both quiet.

The register had it as enforced. It was found by accident, while a neighbouring script was being edited — not by any check, which is the entire argument for the queue.

casualty two · allowed what it blocked

The gate that depended on a tool that wasn't installed

A commit gate — the one whose whole job is to refuse a commit that stages a credential — used a JSON command-line utility for both reading its input and emitting its decision. That utility isn't installed on the machine in question, deliberately. Its wiring swallowed errors so a broken hook could never block real work. Net effect: the gate errored before it evaluated a single commit — one staging a real credential would have passed indistinguishably from a clean one.

Two further latent bugs surfaced during the fix: the denial used a message format the runtime had superseded, and the scan ran from a working directory that wasn't the repository being committed to — so even with the tool present, it would have inspected nothing.

Worse than casualty one in a specific way. That one failed to warn; this one silently allowed. And it was wired but never registered in either artifact — so it read as neither enforced nor tracked, the worst of both. The rewrite inverted its posture: any commit-shaped command it cannot evaluate — interpreter missing, interpreter crashing, target unresolved — now denies. Then it was re-tested twice: once against crafted inputs, once live through the real wiring, including a simulated broken toolchain that the old version waved through.

Two softer findings landed in the same pass, both “the map said enforced, the territory disagreed”: one check was a reminder mislabelled as a gate (the rule it supposedly enforced was narrated all along), and another was present on disk but never wired to any event, so it had never once run. Neither was a fail-open, but both were corrected in the register, because an overclaimed check is a lie about coverage even when the code is fine.

The rule that came out of it. A wired hook is not a tracked check, and a tracked check is not a biting one — only the bite-test closes that gap. The corollary is scheduling: both casualties were caught reactively, by luck. The proactive complement is deliberately not a one-session sprint — a few rows driven to bite-tested per session, reviewed at every release. The queue's job is to keep the residual visible in the meantime, and it says out loud which proofs are still owed.

06

The SOPs I refuse to write yet

A full corporate process suite would cover finance close, HR onboarding, procurement, sales ops, support. All of it is deliberately absent — and listed as absent, with the trigger that earns each one. Documenting the gaps is part of documenting the system: a successor learns as much from what's missing on purpose as from what's present.

Deferred procedureExisting seedWrite it when

The reasoning is the same one that makes the forged-by column mandatory. What makes every procedure in this register credible is that a real event produced it. Writing one for a function nobody runs yet produces a document that reads hollow, buries the ones that matter, and creates exactly the too-much-governance-for-the-headcount smell the register exists to avoid. Write it when the trigger fires, not before.

One row on the reconciliation list has no living successor at all — the finance procedure — and it's marked as genuinely orphaned rather than quietly dropped. A register that only lists what's covered is a marketing document.

Why a solo founder builds this

Because the risk that matters isn't a bug. It's that the working knowledge of how a five-repository system stays correct lives in one person's head, and every convention that survives only by being remembered is a piece of that exposure. Converting a remembered rule into a red check is the only move that reduces it permanently — and unlike documentation, it can be counted.

Which is why the ledger is a separate artifact rather than a section. Ledger A shrinking, and Ledger B graduating to bite-tested, is the literal measure of how much of this operating layer now survives the author leaving. It's the only process metric here I'd defend in a diligence conversation, because it's the only one that can go the wrong way and show it.
