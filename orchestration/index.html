PROCESS · MULTI-SESSION ORCHESTRATION

Workflow design · parallel sessions

One coordinator, several workers, and one shared repository.

Running several AI coding sessions at once is easy to start and hard to keep coherent. The obvious partition — give each session a different subject — is the one that fails, because unrelated subjects keep landing in the same files. The partition that works is by write-surface: a session is handed a repository and a set of path globs, and the globs are checked to be disjoint before anything is spawned.

This is a write-up of three rounds of that pattern, run against a multi-repository workspace by one person. It covers what the leases are, which resources deliberately stay serial, what it cost, and the one defect class that showed up in every round.

The issue count covers rounds 2 and 3. Round 1 has no usable numbers because of how it was run: nearly every step waited on me to approve it, and I came back to approve things whenever I got to it. Those sessions sat idle for most of that round, so its timing and cost don't compare to the two that followed. Taking those gates out is most of what changed between round 1 and round 2.

Nothing here is a product claim. The work itself is described only by shape — a checker, a gate, a test suite. The coordination is the transferable part.

01

Lease by write-surface, not by topic

The failure mode that motivates everything else: two sessions given clearly unrelated jobs, both of which turn out to need the same file. Topic independence does not imply file independence, and you find out at merge time.

To be clear about what is and isn't new here: partitioning work by file ownership is old. Locking schemes, CODEOWNERS, monorepo ownership boundaries and branch policies have all been about this for decades, and none of it was invented for this project. What's specific to the AI-session case is that the workers can't negotiate. Two engineers who both need the same file end up talking to each other, usually before either has finished. Two sessions don't. They both just write it, and the first anyone hears about it is the merge.

the unit

A lease is a repo plus globs

Not “you take auth, you take docs.” A lease is (repository, [path globs]) — a concrete, enumerable set of files a session is permitted to write. Everything outside it is read-only to that session.

the check

Disjointness is verified, not assumed

Before any session is spawned, the glob sets are expanded and intersected. A non-empty intersection is a planning error, caught in a few seconds, rather than a merge conflict discovered after both sessions have finished.

the isolation

One worktree per lease

Each session gets its own git worktree and its own branch. The lease says what a session is allowed to write; the worktree makes it physically impossible for two of them to be editing the same working copy at once.

the consequence

Topics can overlap freely

Once the partition is by file, two sessions are allowed to be thinking about the same subject — which happens more than you would expect, and is fine. What they may not do is write the same line.

Where this leaks. A lease constrains writes. It does nothing about a session reading a file another session is halfway through rewriting, so a worker can build correct work on a premise that's being invalidated underneath it.

Nothing automated catches that. What catches it is the coordinator, reading the diffs as they come back, spotting that two of them disagree about the same assumption, deciding which one is right, and telling the affected lease directly. Scheduling helps as well — two leases known to sit on either side of a shared assumption go in different rounds. Both of those are conventions rather than checks, and the sibling write-up on enforcement is blunt about what a convention is worth. This one is on that ledger, unfixed.

02

The shape of a round

Writes fan out to disjoint surfaces. Everything that can't be done twice funnels back through one point.

A lease carries more than paths

The path globs are the part that prevents collisions. Three other things get assigned at the same time, and the first of them is the one people are most surprised by.

assigned at spawn

A model, per lease

The planning layer picks which model each lease runs on. They are not all the same. A recent round ran the coordinator on Fable 5, two leases on Sonnet 5, two on Opus 5, one on Opus 4.8, one on Haiku 4.5, and one driving a browser.

Mechanical work — renaming, mirroring a config, running a matrix — does not need the expensive model. The lease that has to read a bad premise and argue back does. Sizing that per lease instead of picking one model for the whole round is most of the cost control there is.

assigned at spawn

Permission to fan out

A lease can be granted the right to spawn its own subagents. The tree is deeper than one level when it needs to be, and the lease decides — it is closer to the work than the coordinator is.

The lease still owns the result. Whatever its subagents produce arrives at the coordinator as that lease's output, under that lease's globs, which keeps the collision guarantee intact no matter how wide it went underneath.

assigned at spawn

A scoped pre-authorisation

Dated, revocable, and naming exactly which otherwise-gated files this lease may touch — plus a park and report list for things it may not, and a hard-decline list. A worker that has to stop and ask about every gated file is not running in parallel with anything.

not built yet

A control window

The next thing to try: baking a bounded window of direct control into a lease, so it can drive a surface the coordinator would otherwise have to drive on its behalf. The browser-driving lease above is the first case that wants it.

Written here as intent, not as something that exists.

03

Centralise the serial resources

Some resources cannot be written by two sessions at once without one of them losing. The rule is not “coordinate carefully” — it is route them through one writer and let everything else run free. Four qualify.

The tracking board

Two sessions updating the same board produce last-write-wins, silently. Workers report status to the coordinator; the coordinator writes the board. The cost is a round-trip; the benefit is that the board is never wrong.

Merges to the default branch

Serial for the ordinary reason — each merge changes what the next one is rebasing onto. Also serial for a second reason specific to this workspace: the default branch of one repository publishes on merge, so a merge there is an irreversible outward-facing act and gets a human in the loop.

Shared file sinks

The handful of files that legitimately every worker wants to append to — a changelog, a metrics file, an index. These are precisely the files a topic-based partition collides on. Leasing them to nobody and having the coordinator write them is simpler than any locking scheme.

Work intake

Workers surface findings; they don't file them. Four sessions filing issues independently produces duplicates, inconsistent labels and no dedup, and the person who inherits that mess is the same person who launched the round. Findings come back as text and the coordinator decides what becomes an item.

That triage is itself now a job with a tool behind it rather than something done by hand at the end of a round, which is what closes the loop below.

The test for whether something belongs on this list is not “is it shared” — almost everything is shared. It is: if two sessions did this simultaneously, would the loser know? A merge conflict is loud and therefore safe to leave distributed. A board overwrite is silent, so it gets centralised. That test is the same one that produces section 05.

holding the line

“Holding receipts is not the same as owning the sink”

One worker finished holding better information than the coordinator about a batch of pending merges — the ordering, the evidence, all of it — and was offered the merges. It declined, in those words.

It was right. The value of a single writer is that it is unconditional; an exception granted to whoever currently seems best-informed is not a rule, and the next round has no way to know the exception was ever made.

the coordinator's own failure

Touching a lease's tree mid-run

The coordinator applied an update to a shared file while a worker's test matrix was still running against it. Nothing errored. But there was no way to tell whether the run had read the file before or after the change, so the result was uninterpretable and had to be discarded.

The rule that came out of it is one line: if you must touch a lease's tree, tell it first. The single-writer discipline binds the coordinator too, and this is the case where it forgot.

A related rule, learned the same way. Deciding which files a change could possibly affect, twice, from memory — once by the coordinator and once by a worker — produced two short lists that were both wrong, and both missed the one that mattered. Enumerating instead of recalling took a single command. Enumerate, don't recall is now the standing form, and it is the same instinct as the rest of this page: prefer the thing that reads real state over the thing that sounds right.

What centralising the merges actually buys

The technical argument for one merger is rebase ordering. The argument that matters more in practice is about where the waiting happens.

Run four branches yourself and you are on call for all four. Each one finishes when it finishes, and each one wants you to review it, commit, push, open a PR, wait on checks, merge, then clean up the branch. Four times, at four unpredictable moments, interleaved. The work is trivial and the interruption is not — you can't leave, because something is always about to need a click.

Routing all of it through the coordinator turns that into one decision at the end. The system absorbs the per-branch bookkeeping; the human shows up once, for the worktree, not once per branch. Click, click, click, click becomes click. That is the actual return on the pattern for a single operator, and it is much larger than the throughput gain — round 1 skipped it, gated every step on me, and is the round with no usable numbers precisely because I spent it waiting.

The loop this closes

Intake, leases and merges aren't three separate policies. They're one cycle, and the board is both where it starts and where it ends up.

1The coordinator reads the boardbounded by written rules

It picks up open items and evaluates them against the standing guardrails: the process SOPs, the decision records, the persistent memory, the repo-specific configuration. That context is what makes a slate of work coherent rather than a list of whatever was open.

2It builds the teamone model per lease

Items get grouped by the write surface they need, globs get intersected, and each lease is sized — model, fan-out permission, pre-authorisation. Issues are assigned to whichever lease already owns their files, so one lease may carry several unrelated items.

3Each lease works in its own contextand evaluates on its own terms

A lease sees its own surface in far more depth than the coordinator can. It is expected to disagree with the item it was handed if the item is wrong, and it reports back rather than acting outside its globs.

4The coordinator reconciles what came backthe expensive step

This is the part that doesn't parallelise and the part that costs. Several leases report from several different vantage points, and one reader has to hold all of it at once: which findings are the same finding, which contradict, which change what another lease was told, what actually merges and in what order.

5It writes the boarddeduped, single voice

Findings become items here and only here, filed by the one participant that saw every lease's output. That's the difference between a board that accumulates four sessions' worth of overlapping noise and one that a human can act on — and it is why the triage step got its own tooling instead of staying a chore at the end of a round.

6Which is the input to the next roundback to step 1

Either the same coordinator carries on, or a fresh one starts from the board it just wrote. Nothing has to survive in anyone's head between cycles. That's what makes it a loop instead of one very long session.

04

What three rounds cost

Cost here is percentage points of a rolling weekly allowance on a subscription, read off a usage panel. It is approximate and not metered per session. A whole round consumes single-digit percent of a week.

Why the unit is a weekly allowance and not dollars. Write-ups of multi-agent setups usually assume metered API billing, where the question is cost per call and parallelism is close to free. This runs on a subscription with a shared weekly ceiling, which changes the shape of the problem: the constraint is not per-call price, it's one pool that every lease and the coordinator draw from at once. That is what makes the coordinator's reconciliation step expensive in a way an API-billed setup would not surface the same way — and it is why the pattern is worth measuring in the first place rather than just scaled up.

05

The defect that reports success

Across all three rounds the same class kept turning up, in unrelated parts of the system, found by workers who were not looking for it: a failure whose symptom is a green result rather than an error. It is the most transferable thing on this page, and it is worth stating as a rule — a check that cannot fail loudly will eventually pass quietly.

shape one · checked the wrong thing, reported pass

The gate that scanned a directory nothing was in

A gate ran its scan from a working directory that was not the one being changed. It found no problems there, because there was nothing there. It reported pass, correctly and uselessly, for every run.

Nothing about the output distinguished “scanned and clean” from “scanned an empty place.” Both are the same green. The general fix is to make a check assert that it examined a non-zero amount of something, and fail if it did not.

shape two · passed because the input was empty

The test whose fixture never arrived

A test asserted that a bad input produces no output. It passed. It would also have passed if the tool under test had never run at all — and that is what was happening, because the fixture resolved to nothing.

An assertion of absence is satisfied perfectly by a test that never executed. The counter-measure is structural rather than clever: every “stays quiet” case is paired with a “makes noise” case over the same wiring. If the noisy twin is silent too, the harness is dead, and now you can tell.

shape three · read as done, did nothing

The merge that appeared to close three items and closed none

A pull request body listed the items it resolved using a shorthand that reads naturally to a person and matches nothing at all to the platform parsing it. The PR merged. The summary read as though three items had been closed. Three items stayed open, and nobody looked again, because the message said they were handled.

This one is the purest instance of the class: no component failed. The merge succeeded, the text was accurate English, and the outcome was silently nothing. It was caught later by a sweep that reconciles merged pull requests against the items they claimed to close — which exists now precisely because reading the PR body was not enough.

Why an orchestration round is unusually good at finding these. A worker arrives with no memory of why anything was built and no stake in it being correct. It reads a check the way a stranger does, and a stranger is the only reader who notices that a pass proves nothing. The same blindness that makes a session useless for judgement calls — no context — is what makes it excellent at this specific job. Three of the rounds' most useful findings came from a worker declining to believe a green.

06

The instruction that reads fine and cannot be executed

Rounds 1 and 2 asked every worker to check in at fixed points. Across both rounds, the number of check-ins received was zero. Round 3 asked four workers for the same thing and all four complied, unprompted. Same coordinator, same model, same brief structure. The difference was one paragraph.

rounds 1–2 — 0 check-ins received

“Reply to the sender of this message for all checkpoints — do not ask for a session id.”

Why it cannot be run. The brief is delivered as the worker's opening prompt. There is no sender. The instruction names an affordance that does not exist in the situation it is describing — and the second clause forbids the one route that does work. It is not vague; it is precise about the wrong thing.

round 3 — 4 of 4 checked in

“To reach me, use the cross-session message tool. Find my session id by listing sessions — I am the one titled <coordinator>. Your brief arrived as your opening prompt, so there is no sender to reply to.”

Why it runs. It names the mechanism, gives a way to derive the address rather than assuming one, and pre-empts the wrong inference explicitly. It is the same request; it is now executable.

The fix turns on a small asymmetry that had to be discovered: a session cannot look up its own id, but it can be found by others under its title. So the address could never be supplied by the coordinator — it had to be made derivable by the worker. The earlier briefs had been written by a coordinator that was itself reachable by reply, and had never tested the return path from a session spawned the way these were.

The uncomfortable part. That paragraph was reviewed before it was sent, twice, and read as perfectly clear — because it is clear, to a reader. The defect was invisible from the writing side and only showed up as an absence, and absences are easy to miss. Same class as section 05: a brief that reads fine and does nothing is a plausible green. The standing assumption now is that an unexecuted instruction is under-specified rather than ignored.

The general form: an instruction is real only if it names an observable trigger, a mechanism, and an address the reader can work out. Hand a human an impossible instruction and they ask a clarifying question. A worker session does something worse. It forms a reasonable belief that the channel doesn't exist, and proceeds without it.

the correction · and who made it

The diagnosis was also wrong, and a worker said so

The coordinator's first conclusion was that the reply path was structurally impossible — no sender, therefore no channel. A worker corrected it: “There was a channel. I didn't find it. Don't record ‘structurally impossible’ — that would fix the playbook in the wrong direction.”

It had reached for the tool whose name made it the obvious first guess — the one that addresses subagents a session spawns itself, not sibling sessions — been told no such recipient was reachable, and reasonably concluded no path existed. The real defect was narrower and more fixable than the coordinator's version of it, and getting it right mattered: the wrong diagnosis would have produced a workaround instead of a working address.

This was not unusual. Across the three rounds, workers corrected the coordinator's premise more often than the reverse — which is the ordinary asymmetry between a lead and the person actually in the file, not a scoreboard — and the briefs came to say so out loud, granting explicit permission to reject the task's own stated reasoning. In round 3 four workers corrected the premise of their own assignment and two corrected the coordinator's. That is not a failure of the pattern; it is the pattern's error-correction path working, and it is the strongest argument for writing the brief so it can be argued with.

07

Blind spots emit a burst, then go quiet

A worker pointed at an area that has never been checked comes back with a pile of findings. The instinct is to read that as a pile of problems. It is usually one unlit area, and the pile is what a first light looks like.

the pattern

Burst, then silence

The first pass over an unmeasured surface returns a cluster of related findings. The second pass over the same surface returns very little. Nothing was fixed in between except the surface being observable.

the inference

Count areas, not items

A cluster of findings that share a root is one item of information, not N. Treating them as N inflates the apparent problem and, worse, produces N tracked tickets that all close for the same reason.

the consequence

This is why it converges

If findings were independent, each round would uncover as many as the last and the backlog would grow forever. Because they cluster by unlit area, and areas are finite, successive rounds return less. The pattern terminates.

the caution

A quiet surface is ambiguous

Silence means the area is clean or that the thing looking at it is broken. That is section 05 again, one level up — so a quiet result is only trusted from a checker that has been shown to go red on a known-bad input.

Which sets up the last rule, and the one that saves the most work over time.

→Prefer the enforcing check over fixing N instancesthe leverage rule

Given a burst of related findings, there are two responses: fix each one, or build the check that makes the whole class impossible. The second is almost always right, and the reason is what happens to each afterwards.

A tracked item accumulates forever. It sits in a backlog, gets re-read at every triage, gets re-prioritised, and costs attention every time someone scans the list — whether or not it is ever done. A check self-exits. Once it is green and wired to run automatically, it stops needing to be thought about at all; it has removed itself from the attention budget while continuing to do the work.

So the preferred output of a worker is not a list of corrections. It is one check, plus the corrections it forced — and a demonstration that the check goes red without them.

→Assert the state, don't pattern-match the intentthe design that came out of it

The best example came from the merge that closed nothing. The assignment specified a check that would scan pull-request text before merge and warn on the malformed shorthand. The worker refused the specified design and built the opposite: a check that runs after the merge and compares the items the text claimed to close against the items actually closed.

Its reasoning is the durable part — “a matcher guesses intent, an assertion reads real state, and only one of those has a false-positive class.” The evidence backed it: run against several hundred historical merges, the naive matcher produced 12 hits of which 3 were real. Among the false positives were a match that read a closure out of a heading which explicitly said the item stays open, and a match on a sentence written specifically to say the opposite. Meanwhile the state assertion caught three distinct malformed shapes without being told any of them existed, because it never had to know what a closing phrase looks like.

The rule this produced applies well beyond merges: a warning that cries wolf gets muted within a week, and then the real one is invisible too. A noisy check is not a partial win over no check — past some false-positive rate it is worse than nothing, because it trains the reader to ignore the channel.

08

Where this goes, if it goes anywhere

Everything above happened. This part hasn't been tried, and it is written down here as intent so that it can be wrong in public rather than quietly abandoned.

?A coordinator that runs its own next rounduntried

Step 6 of the loop hands the board to whoever starts the next cycle, and there is no particular reason that has to be a person. A coordinator could finish its round, evaluate its own worktree the same way it evaluates a lease's, pull the next set of non-intersecting items off the board it just wrote, and go again. Come back later to several worktrees staged and waiting rather than one.

The obvious failure mode is the one this whole page is about: a cycle that runs clean and produces nothing, repeatedly, with no one reading the output. It would need the stopping rule before it needed the automation.

?Something above the coordinatorsuntried

Past some number of concurrent coordinators, the merge problem comes back one level up — several finished worktrees, each internally consistent, that now have to land together in an order nobody has worked out. That is the same problem as section 03 with bigger units, and it presumably wants the same answer: one writer, holding the ordering.

Whether that tier is worth building depends entirely on whether the cost shape in section 04 holds at that scale, and there is no reason to assume it does. The reconciliation step is the expensive one, and a tier whose entire job is reconciliation is not obviously a bargain.

What I'd keep, and what I'd change

Keep the lease. Partitioning by write-surface rather than by topic removed an entire category of failure, and the disjointness check that makes it work costs seconds. Keep the serial spine too: the discipline of routing the board, the merges, the shared sinks and the intake through one writer is what stops N parallel sessions from producing N inconsistent versions of the truth.

Change the measurement. The most interesting question — where the coordinator's effort actually went — has no data behind it. Cost was read off a usage panel rather than metered per session; per-worker attribution was never aggregated; working time was never separated from elapsed time, which is why the round-level speedup figure had to be thrown out. Three rounds in, the pattern is better understood than it is instrumented, and the next round's first job is to fix that rather than to run wider.

Spend the coordinator on the right thing. The correction data and the coordinator's own retrospective point the same way — the cost figure cannot corroborate it, since it never resolved to a per-session share. Workers corrected the coordinator's premise more often than the reverse, and the coordinator's own retrospective landed on the conclusion that follows from that: its highest-value act is escalating and verifying, not deciding. Its most reliable failure mode was also specific and cheap to fix — inferring from absence instead of reading. Every instance was one command away from being checked.

And treat every green as a claim. The recurring finding across all three rounds was not a bug in any component — it was a check, a test, a merge, and a written instruction that each reported success while doing nothing. The failure that looks like an error gets fixed. The failure that looks like a pass gets built on.
