FIELD NOTES / MULTI-SESSION ORCHESTRATION

I built a harness for running several Claude sessions at once, then spent twenty-eight rounds grading it.

THE OBSERVATION WINDOW
28rounds
102 worker sessions
One shared repository set
Figures kept when they got worse

The coordination was the easy part. Each session gets a list of files it may write. Nothing else may write there. Anything that cannot safely happen twice (merges, the tracking board, the shared append-only files, filing issues) goes through one place. Other people have built this. Section 03, and it is short.

Grading was the hard part, and most of this page is about that. I changed one rule about what counted as a mistake and the measured rate went from 0.8 to 8.3 per session, with no change to the work being measured. The figure I quoted most often was wrong by one, because the session being graded was also the session doing the grading. My best inter-rater agreement was 49 out of 49, and it cannot be trusted. I published the reason beside it.

01

Grading the work, and then grading the grading

For twenty-four rounds I counted mistakes with no rubric: nothing written down for what counted as one, who was allowed to decide, or which rounds a number could fairly be compared against. Every figure in section 02 came out of that. The rubric arrived at round 24, and the first thing it did was move my headline number by a factor of ten.

The admission rule moved the number ten-fold, and the work did not change

Until round 24, something reached the tally if a session filed it under the heading the tally was watching for. So I was counting headings. The number read as a measurement of the round and it was a measurement of what people remembered to label: the same defect as everything in section 02, one level up.

Near-misses reaching the tallyrecordedworkersper worker
Round 23 — intake keyed on the label560.8
Round 24 — intake keyed on the shape2538.3

A factor of exactly ten, and it holds to four decimal places. The rule that replaced it has four yes-or-no questions. Was a claim made? Was something about it wrong, or unable to carry what it was used for? Can you point at where? Is it already counted somewhere else? Yes-or-no on purpose, because two graders almost never mean the same thing by a 3. There is also a required answer of insufficient evidence, so a grader who has to guess says so instead of producing a guess that reads like a judgment.

Said beside the figure, or it gets read as something it is not. The numerator counts every admitted near-miss regardless of who produced it, including the coordinator's own, while the denominator contains no coordinator at all. So “per worker” is a detection-posture index, not a per-person error rate. It did not revert. The next round came in between 8 and 9. But no claim of stability survives either: three rounds, three rubric versions, and a single added admission item moved the figure by 0.4.

The most-quoted figure in a round was wrong by one, because the graded party was the grader

Round 24 recorded that 18 of its 33 scored instances were the coordinator's own errors: the number that led the round's summary, fed the per-worker error rate, and went to the ledger. The coordinator classified all 33. A separate closing session re-derived the attribution independently and found 19. The one it had missed was a guard the coordinator itself had written and broadcast to all three workers as a control; that entry's own text says it is the coordinator's, and it appears in no enumeration of the coordinator's errors anywhere in the round.

The fair reading is less satisfying than the story. That coordinator scored several of its own items that a strict reading would have routed out of the count altogether; every borderline call it made ran toward scoring itself. The defect was an omission under load, which is exactly what an independence rule is for and exactly what diligence is not. The wrong number was not overwritten. It is still on the ledger, still in the round's summary, still in what the tool prints, with the correction beside it. Editing it would tidy away the fact that it was ever wrong.

→The rule, and the one line that enforces itnarrated, then enforced

Any party records freely, including against itself, immediately, self-reporting is the most productive habit in the series and nothing slows it down. No party sets the score on an entry it owns. A separate closing seat classifies, and does not classify its own either; those route to the one human in the loop. The mechanical form is a single comparison: the party that classified an entry must not be the party that owns it, on any entry that scored. The rubric calls that the one check that would have caught the round's headline.

A line through 97%, 89% and 100% is a line through three rulers. The middle figure is not a decline and the last is not a recovery. Two other rounds could have produced a figure and declined to, saying why: one because a single grader had classified everything, so there was no second rating to agree with and none would be manufactured; the other because it had re-read five entries out of ninety-two, and a partial agreement presented as a rate is this series' own defect.

49 of 49
100% agreement, and it should not be quoted. The first grader had written its per-row justification into the very field a second grader must read to know what the row is. The re-derivation was anchored by construction and could not be un-anchored afterward. It is worse than that: the second grader's own brief named exactly where the first had been least confident. What that measures is whether the calls are defensible. It is not evidence that they are independently reproducible.
the fix is cheap and belongs in the tooling · strip the classification and its reasons before handing a set to a second reader · and record whether the pass was blind

Three rows in that round were genuinely blind, because the first grader had declined to classify entries it owned. No agreement figure exists for those three, and they are reported separately rather than folded into the 49, the same move as leaving the wrong 18 standing. A number that has to be explained away is worth more than one that has to be taken on trust, but only if the explanation ships attached to it.

A prediction registered five times, missed in every available direction, then retired

From round 20 the defect rate was registered as a band before the round existed, written by the previous round's coordinator and committed before any worker was spawned, so the coordinator executing it started cold and could not have shaped its own bar. A scoping clause made it falsifiable: only instances found incidentally, while doing other work, count.

Five rounds were scored. One landed in band. The others failed high on a unit that turned out to be wrong, low on the unit that replaced it, high again on that replacement a round later, and once produced no number at all, the same night's work returning 6.40 or 2.67 on one agreed denominator depending only on how finely instances were counted, with nothing in the series saying which was right. That round sealed unscoreable rather than picking one.

why it was retired, and why that is not a retreat

A band missed high and low on adjacent rounds is not a bar you are failing; it is an instrument you are not reading. And the miss that mattered was scored against a round whose detection instrument had changed underneath it, the ten-fold intake change above. The falsifier registered at open ranged over four declared attributes. The intake rule was not one of them, because a falsifier only ranges over what somebody thought to list.

So the band was measured before being dropped. Across the manifests it is written under eight distinct key spellings and read by nothing: no hook, no checker, no field of that kind in the ledger at all. A field with no consumer has nothing holding its name still. Retired: predicting a range and grading a round against it. Kept: the admission rule, the classification rules, grader independence, and the version stamp. Those make an observation comparable and keep a party from grading itself. A predicted range does neither.

The ruling that retired it came with a ratio nobody enjoyed publishing, over one recent round the series had written 2.4× more prose about itself than product, and several of that round's headline findings were defects in machinery whose only customer was the machinery. And the first pass at that ratio was wrong, in the series' own registered failure mode: the window had no fixed endpoint, so it ran to the current tip and counted the commits being written to record the ruling. A counter whose window is opened by an external caller measures the caller. On a figure about measurement discipline, in the decision that retired a measurement.

Full tables. Every scored round, both denominators, every audit that moved a published figure against the party that recorded it, are in the appendix.

02

The check that is narrower than its claim

This is what I was counting. The same kind of defect turned up in every round, in unrelated parts of the system, found by sessions that were not looking for it: a failure whose symptom is a green result instead of an error. A check that cannot fail loudly will eventually pass quietly.

shape one · checked the wrong thing, reported pass

The gate that scanned a directory nothing was in

A gate ran its scan from a working directory that was not the one being changed. It found no problems there, because there was nothing there. It reported pass on every run, correctly, and told me nothing. The output for “scanned and clean” and the output for “scanned an empty place” were the same bytes. The fix is to make the check assert it examined a non-zero amount of something, and fail when it did not.

shape two · passed because the input was empty

The test whose fixture never arrived

A test asserted that a bad input produces no output. It passed. It would have passed just as well if the tool had never run, and that is what was happening, because the fixture resolved to nothing. A test that never executes satisfies an assertion of absence perfectly. So every “stays quiet” case now has a “makes noise” case beside it, over the same wiring. When the noisy one goes silent too, the harness is dead and you can see it.

the hard version · the defect inside its own fix

An assertion that was its own witness

A test asserted a protection existed by searching for a literal string, on a line that itself contained that string. Delete the protection and the search still matches itself. Then the fix did the same thing. The session built a detector for the whole class, plus a control to prove the detector worked. The control carried its own copy of the pattern, so what it proved was that a detector of this shape can fire. It never touched the real one. Breaking the real detector left the control green. This was caught because that session mutation-tested a control nothing required it to mutation-test.

Knowing about the class does not protect you from it, and round 25 put a number on that. Eight instances of one family, a check returning a confident number over the wrong subject, from four different sessions, all four of which had read the rule naming that family. In all eight cases the thing that caught it was an assertion that made the failing case loud: a floor on how much subject the check had to examine, a coverage check run in both directions, a grep for failure words instead of success words, a row count that would not reconcile. The rule caught none of the eight.

this page, being edited · both checks broke because of the edit

Two checks that failed for the reason they were run

Editing this page down, I replaced most of its em-dashes with full stops. Then I ran two checks to confirm no figure had been dropped in the process. Both reported losses. Both were wrong, and both were wrong because of the edit they were checking.

The first compared a window of text around every number, before and against after. Repunctuating a sentence moves that window, so the check reported four sentences missing that were sitting in the file, repunctuated. It was measuring my punctuation and reporting it as content.

The second counted every numeric token with the pattern (?<![\d,.])NUM(?![\d,.]). That trailing exclusion means a number followed by a full stop does not match. Converting em-dashes to full stops is precisely what moves numbers to the ends of sentences, so five figures stopped matching and were reported gone. The check had a blind spot, and the edit under test was what created it.

Raw byte counts settled it, because they do not care what is next to a number: 29 was 11 before and 11 after, 176,531 was 2 and 2, 49 was 14 and 14. Nothing had been lost.

What makes this one worth keeping is the timing. A check whose coverage is narrowed by the very change it is verifying will fail at the one moment it is being relied on, and it fails by producing output that looks like a finding. I wrote both of these while editing the page that is about this, and I believed the first one for several minutes.

Why running sessions in parallel is good at finding these. A fresh session has no memory of why anything was built and no stake in it being correct. It reads a check the way a stranger does, and a stranger is who notices that a pass proves nothing. The same blindness that makes a session bad at judgment calls makes it good at this one job.

A dozen more worked instances: a docstring promising what its assertion structurally cannot deliver, a provenance stamp certifying numbers it never opens, a liveness probe answering about the wrong process namespace, five capabilities built and correct and unreachable, a merge that read as closing three items and closed none, are in the appendix.

03

The mechanism, which is the ordinary part

None of this is new. Locking schemes, CODEOWNERS and monorepo ownership boundaries have partitioned work by file ownership for decades. The one thing specific to AI sessions is that they cannot negotiate with each other. Two engineers who both need the same file talk about it first. Two sessions both write it, and the merge is where I find out.

the unit

A lease is a repo plus globs

Not “you take auth, you take docs.” A lease is a repository plus a list of path globs: an enumerable set of files that session may write. Everything else is read-only to it. Each lease gets its own git worktree and its own branch.

the check

Disjointness is verified, not assumed

Before any session starts, the glob sets get expanded and intersected. A non-empty intersection is a planning error and it costs seconds to find. The same collision found after both sessions finish is a merge conflict.

the spine

One writer for everything serial

The tracking board, merges to the default branch, shared append-only files, and filing issues. The test I use is one question: if two sessions did this at the same time, would the loser find out? A merge conflict is loud, so it can stay distributed. A board overwrite is silent, so it goes through one writer.

what it buys

One decision instead of four

Run four branches yourself and you are on call for all four. Through one writer it becomes one decision at the end. Click, click, click, click becomes click. Working alone, that is worth more to me than the throughput is.

Here is where it leaks. A lease controls writes. It says nothing about a session reading a file another session is halfway through rewriting, so a session can do correct work on a premise that is being invalidated underneath it. Two sessions can hold completely disjoint globs and still break each other: one changes a constant or a threshold inside a file only it holds, and the other's files read or assert against that value. Both merge clean. No lease is violated. Nothing fires. My check answers whether two sessions wrote the same bytes. The question I needed answered was whether one session invalidated a baseline another one was reading.

rounds 28 and 29 · the caveat is what made the instance findable

Filed with the hole in its own evidence stated

I wrote that gap up at round 28's close as a tracked item. The filing said, in those words, that the one measured instance I had involved a shared file, which is the easy version that a path check could in principle see, that there was no measured instance of the pure form, and that it should not be quoted as one.

Writing the caveat down is why an instance exists. The next round's coordinator went looking for the pure form in its own governing document and found one: that document's justification for running three lanes at once argued entirely from disjoint write surfaces, and stated that the single seam anyone had identified was removed. Removing that item removed the file overlap. The semantic dependency was still there.

The shape, which is all that transfers. One feature's only call site pulls an event predicate and a text-shaping helper from the first lane's surface, hands the result to a downstream function owned by the second, from a component owned by the third. The contract between them is a shared formatting convention, written as two separate expressions in two files belonging to two different lanes. No two lanes share a path. All three branches would merge clean and nothing would fire.

The qualification travels with it. This is a predicted break. No lease had started, and I checked that rather than assuming it. What I have is a measured contract across measured-disjoint surfaces, with the failure modes traced to source. No defect landed. Round 29 was still running when this page was published, and nothing here reports its outcome. What it does establish: the pure form showed up in the first round anyone looked for it, inside the document that asserted the isolation.

Ownership is recorded as shared, and the split is the part worth reading. The sentence was written at prep by the same seat that had filed the issue one turn earlier. The coordinator read it, relied on it to start three lanes, and restated the argument in a document of its own without checking it. The author offered to take the whole thing. The coordinator declined, because inheriting a claim and then repeating it in your own document means you have made it too.

04

Instructions that read fine and cannot be executed

Rounds 1 and 2 asked every session to check in at fixed points. Across both rounds I received zero check-ins. Round 3 asked four sessions for the same thing and got four, unprompted. Same coordinator, same model, same brief structure. One paragraph was different.

rounds 1–2 — 0 check-ins received

“Reply to the sender of this message for all checkpoints, do not ask for a session id.”

Why it cannot be run. The brief arrives as the session's opening prompt. There is no sender to reply to. The instruction names something that does not exist in the situation it is describing, and the second half forbids the one route that would have worked. The sentence is perfectly precise about the wrong thing.

round 3 — 4 of 4 checked in

“To reach me, use the cross-session message tool. Find my session id by listing sessions. I am the one titled . Your brief arrived as your opening prompt, so there is no sender to reply to.”

Why it runs. It names the mechanism, gives the session a way to work out the address for itself, and says out loud which wrong inference to skip. Same request, now executable.

The general form: an instruction is real when it names an observable trigger, a mechanism, and an address the reader can work out. Hand a person an impossible instruction and they ask you about it. A session does something worse. It concludes, reasonably, that the channel does not exist, and carries on without it. I reviewed that paragraph twice before sending it and it read as perfectly clear, because it is clear to a reader. From the writing side the defect is invisible. It shows up as an absence.

Writing the rule down is the weakest thing I can do

Round 27 asked the harder question. When an instruction is executable and its premise is stale, what actually catches it? I measured both candidate answers inside one round.

Two ways to stop an instruction being acted on wronglyinstancescatches
A written rule, addressed to the party that might get it wrong80
A receiver checking a premise against repository state before acting44

The reason is structural. The coordinator is the party most likely to be wrong about state, because it sits furthest from the code and its instructions are stalest by the time they arrive. Trying harder does not fix staleness on the sending side. On the receiving side it costs one command. Every premise caught that round was falsifiable in a single invocation. Nothing that got caught required judgement, and nothing that required judgement got caught.

The eighth written rule is project memory, and the worker asked for it to be counted. A lease re-derived, mid-task, a lesson the persistent memory had carried verbatim for two weeks, specific to this project, with a first-person cost attached. It did not reach the party at the moment of need; they found it at close-out while deduplicating, after independently re-deriving it. The surface everyone assumes is exempt, our own prior finding addressed to us, failed the same way the other seven did. It was added to the count at the worker's request, on the stated ground that it makes the ratio worse and the clause stronger.

The duty and the channel are one control. Twice that round a worker stopped and asked instead of acting, and both times that was possible only because it had an inbox and a coordinator that answered. The previous round's workers had neither (spawned as in-process subagents, where cross-session messaging is unavailable) so all parties inherited one session identity, a mid-round ruling could not reach them, and the ledger could not tell them apart. Physical isolation held; what was lost was the ability to prove it. A receiver expected to check a premise, with no way to report what it found, converts a caught error into an unlogged one.

Two disclosures the clause carries about itself. The coordinator that recommended it deliberately did not adopt it, a party writing a protocol clause about its own failure mode should not also be the party that enacts it. And measured again the next round, receiver checks came in at four of four with two of the four performed by the seat reporting the figure; that record's own prose says three, and the list of four beside it names two. Writing a rule down does not make it bind, which the clause states about itself, on the ground that a clause about written rules failing cannot exempt itself.

05

What it cost, and how far to trust those numbers

Rounds 1–6 are percentage points of a rolling weekly allowance read off a panel by hand. From round 7 the instrument is output tokens from the transcripts. Nothing in one converts into anything in the other, and this page never does that arithmetic.

I found the measuring tool wrong twice, and neither time by looking for it. First, its window picked sessions but never filtered the messages inside them. That is harmless for a worker session, which starts when its chip does, and wrong for the coordinator, whose session starts before the round and runs past the end of it. After the fix, round 6 fell 5.0 points and round 5 rose 2.4. The correction moves in both directions, which is what makes it a correction. A fix that only ever shrank the number would be a discount, and a discount that flatters whoever applied it is not a finding. Second, its join excluded any session whose first message predated the window instead of testing whether it had messages inside it. So one round resolved 0 of 3 workers against its own manifest. Guarded against being windowed to zero, unguarded against being excluded to zero: section 02's shape, inside the instrument that measures section 05.

What those corrections bought me was not accuracy. Later, hitting a join it could not make, the tool exited non-zero, printed its unresolved rows, and refused to print the coordinator share at all, on the recorded ground that a share computed over half a denominator is arithmetically fine and means nothing. An earlier version of the same tool would have printed a number. The thing that improved across twenty-eight rounds is the tool's willingness to return nothing. That is the harder property and it is what makes the rest of the table worth reading.

The third failure is about when the number was taken. Two rounds published a figure captured before the round had ended. Round 24's closed-window value is 26.8% higher than what it published, located afterwards by bisection. Round 25's was taken 65 seconds early, and I proved that by re-running the unchanged tool against a copy of the manifest with the end time rewound, which reproduced the published numbers exactly. Same tool, same data, right answer at the wrong instant. Both figures stay as published, marked, instead of being quietly swapped. The rule I took from it: a cost figure has to carry the instant it was taken, not the day.

There is exactly one comparison I can make here. Coordinator share only compares across rounds with the same worker count, because the denominator moves with it. Five rounds ran three workers on the same tool: 38.7 · 39.0 · 30.9 · 41.1 · 41.4. An earlier version of this page leaned on the 39.0-to-30.9 step as its one clean comparison. It was clean, it was one excursion, and two later rounds put the figure back where it started. I am recording that correction here instead of deleting the sentence that needed it.

From round 26 I stopped measuring cost. The three most recent rounds carry no coordinator share, and the last two carry no merged-PR figure either. That was deliberate (keep the guards, cut the metrics) and it is still a gap. Those three rounds cannot go in either cost table, and nothing here is evidence that the cost shape held through them.

Is there a standard for this? No.

DORA: deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore (Forsgren, Humble, Kim, Accelerate), measures outcomes, not cost per unit of output. SPACE (Forsgren, Storey, Maddila, Zimmermann, Houck, Butler; ACM Queue 19(1), 2021) argues productivity cannot be captured in a single metric and that activity counts mislead: the most citable thing in the area argues against the number people ask for, and that applies to every figure here. Agentic benchmarks report cost per resolved instance, the closest published analog, but that is a reporting convention assuming a fixed task distribution, and production work is not that. The defensible sentence is the narrow one: these specific things were measured, those are the nearest frameworks, and no conformance is claimed to any of them.

The measure I would keep is human decisions per unit of delivered work. I count it, the system does not, so the system cannot make it look better. One round ran three hours and twenty minutes with no blocking human decisions. There is a more impressive version of that sentence and it is false. “No blocking decisions” is not “no intervention.” I surfaced one substantive finding neither session produced, and I caught the coordinator's merge error through an observation channel only I had. That is detection, and it happened after the fact. I am not going to tell you what any of this replaced. How long this would have taken a person is a number nobody measured.

Both cost tables, all twenty-eight rounds, every footnote about window rules and undercounting, are in the appendix.

What I'd keep, and what I'd change

Grade the grader, and say when the grading was not blind. The rubric sets what gets admitted, how it is classified, who is allowed to classify it, and which version the number was taken under. On first use it moved my headline measure ten-fold with no change to the work. Then it caught the most-quoted figure in a round being wrong by one, because the party being graded was the grader. My best agreement number is 49 of 49 and it cannot be trusted. Publishing a perfect score with the reason to distrust it attached is the only version of that number worth anything.

Measure before believing it, and publish the correction whichever way it goes. The measuring tool was wrong twice and mistimed twice more. Corrections that moved a figure up got published at the worse number. Two consecutive independent audits moved published figures against the interest of whoever recorded them. The wrong values are still on the ledger with the corrections beside them, because editing a landed number tidies away the fact that it was wrong.

Retire a measurement out loud, with the grounds. Predicting a band and grading a round against it was registered five times and landed once. I retired it, and I measured the grounds first: the band was written under eight different key spellings and read by nothing. A field with no consumer has nothing holding its name still. The prediction went. The rubric underneath it stayed, and it had been doing the work the whole time.

Keep the lease, and stop treating path disjointness as sufficient. Partitioning by write-surface removed an entire category of failure and the check costs seconds. But paths can be disjoint while content collides, and the passing check says nothing about that. The gap was filed with the hole in its own evidence stated, and that caveat is what made an instance findable one round later, inside the document asserting the isolation. Keep the serial spine too: routing the board, the merges, the shared sinks and the intake through one writer is what stops N sessions producing N versions of the truth.

Treat every green as a claim. The thing that recurred across twenty-eight rounds was never a bug in a component. It was a test, a stamp, a process kill, a merge, a documentation page and a written instruction, each reporting success while doing nothing. A failure that looks like an error gets fixed. A failure that looks like a pass gets built on. My detector for it is still a session with no memory of why the check was written, reading it like a stranger.
